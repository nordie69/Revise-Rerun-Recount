## Summary

The manuscript is a single-case numerical modelling study of the Mogangling rock landslide on the Dadu River (Luding County, Sichuan), triggered by the 1786 Kangding earthquake. The pre-failure topography is restored by straightening contour lines of a UAV-derived DEM, and the event is reproduced with the three-dimensional discrete element code 3DEC (25345 blocks of 8.7 m by 8.7 m in plan, Mohr-Coulomb blocks and joints, viscous and free-field boundaries), driven by the acceleration record of the 2008 Wenchuan earthquake at Luding station. Kinetic, potential and dissipated energy (KE, PE, DE, with DE defined as the loss of mechanical energy since the start) are computed for the whole sliding mass and for ten monitored blocks in four position groups; effective collisions are detected as local peaks of block velocity against travel distance with a prominence threshold of 30 % (1,139 collisions); and the dispersion of the mass is quantified by alpha-shape envelopes at ten times through two indices, volume swelling (VS) and area growth (AG). Key numbers: field volume about 2400 × 10⁴ m³ against a model sliding mass of 317563 m³, simulated duration 131 s, maximum velocity 31.6 m/s at 12 s, and a "solid to fluidized" transition placed at VS 29.47 % and AG 319.5 %. The main conclusions are energy conservation for the whole mass and non-conservation for single blocks, more collisions and energy transfer for the front (lower) mass, and fragmentation as the cause of envelope growth.

## Strengths

1. The energy terms are defined explicitly per block in Eqs. (1) to (6), with the abbreviations KE, PE, DE and ME used identically in text and figures (PP-14); this makes the energy bookkeeping fully checkable, which is what allowed the closure of the balance to be traced to its definition (GB-33).
2. The collision threshold is shown on one velocity record at five prominence thresholds, 10 % to 50 %, in Fig. 5b to 5f, with text, caption and panels consistent in number and values (PP-17); this makes the threshold dependence of the collision count visible to the reader instead of hiding it (GB-22).
3. The figures carry measurable geometry that is internally consistent: scale bars in Figs. 2 and 3c are proportional, the section a-a' in Fig. 2c matches the profile axis of Fig. 3b, the dimensions annotated in Fig. 4 equal those in the text, and the colour-bar maxima of Fig. 7 equal the stage velocities in Section 3.1 (PP-10, PP-11, PP-13, PP-24); a reader can therefore cross-check model geometry and velocities between figures and text.
4. The monitoring design places ten blocks in four position groups with coordinates and volumes (Table 2), and the numbering of these blocks is consistent across Table 2, Fig. 6, Fig. 9 and Fig. 10a (PP-20); several limitations (no instrumental record of the 1786 event, unknown structural planes, smooth restored terrain) are disclosed where the affected method is introduced (PP-07), so the starting points for position-resolved and sensitivity analyses are already in place.

## Weaknesses

1. The central energy results are partly fixed by construction or rest on inconsistent inputs: the constant energy total follows from the definition of DE in Eq. (4) and therefore shows neither conservation for the whole mass nor non-conservation for single blocks (GB-33), the PE datum stated in the Methods contradicts Figs. 8 and 9 (GB-26), and the stated volumes, dimensions and energy magnitudes cannot be reconciled with each other (GB-15). Suggestion: compute DE from independent terms (contact friction, damping, boundary radiation, seismic input work), state one consistent volume budget and PE datum, and rephrase the Abstract and Conclusion 3 accordingly.
2. The comparative and mechanistic claims about collisions, energy transfer and fragmentation are not shown by the reported results: there is no position-resolved collision or energy-transfer result behind the Abstract and Conclusion 4 (GB-36), the velocity-peak detection is not validated against contact data and its population is undefined (GB-22, GB-24), fragmentation is not measured although Conclusion 5 concludes it (GB-38), and the VS and AG thresholds of the phase transition are not taken at the kinetic energy peak of the run (GB-40). Suggestion: report collisions and energy received per monitored block and position group, normalised by path length, validate the detection against contact forces, and add a fragmentation measure or restrict the conclusions to dispersion.
3. The validity of the single simulation is asserted rather than tested: the field comparison is qualitative only and Conclusion 2 says it "ensures" agreement (GB-31), the failure stages are explained by structural planes that the model explicitly ignores (GB-30), the substitute seismic input is neither justified with sources nor varied (GB-19), and the settings needed to rebuild the model are missing (GB-18, GB-13). Suggestion: compare the final simulated surface quantitatively with the UAV DEM, add sensitivity runs for input motion and discontinuity parameters, and give a full model set-up table.
4. Positioning and reporting fall short in several moderate points: an earlier study by the first and third authors already reports the collision criterion and the main energy findings without being cited (GB-08), four studies of the same landslide in the reference list are never cited (GB-09, GB-42), sixteen in-text citations have no entry (GB-05), the aims exceed what one case and one run can show (GB-02), and data and code are withheld on request (GB-41); key quantities carry several names (GB-11, GB-23, GB-28). Suggestion: delineate the contribution against Ge et al. (2021) and the Mogangling literature, complete the reference list, narrow the aims, deposit data and code in a public repository, and fix one name per quantity.
