## Summary

The manuscript is a single-site observational characterization of the MC fault zone in the Rotondo granite of the Bedretto Underground Laboratory, at about 1 km depth, carried out to prepare the fault activation experiments of the FEAR project. In two boreholes (BO5, 216 m; BO6, 55 m), eleven intervals of 0.73 m were tested with a double-straddle packer system (pulse, constant rate and shut-in tests evaluated with nSIGHTS, type curves, Cooper and Jacob and Horner; flow dimensions after the generalized radial flow model), complemented by optical and acoustic televiewer, full wave sonic and caliper logs, a porosity log derived from Vp with Gardner's relation, and two crosshole tests and step-rate injectivity tests from another campaign. In the laboratory, eleven plugs from BO5 were tested for permeability (pulse decay or steady state) at 15, 25 and 32 MPa effective stress, together with water immersion and helium porosity, P-wave velocity, XRD and CT. The interval means stated for the field range from 5.6 × 10⁻¹⁶ to 1.6 × 10⁻¹¹ m², the laboratory values from 2.0 × 10⁻¹⁹ to 2.0 × 10⁻¹³ m², the crosshole values are 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁵ and 3.9 × 10⁻¹⁵ m², and the flow dimensions lie between 1.58 and 1.95. The authors conclude that laboratory tests underestimate in-situ permeability because they exclude large fractures, that the fault core is the main flow path without a continuous low-permeability core, and that the fault zone combines localized and distributed conduit behaviour.

## Strengths

1. The same boreholes are characterized with field packer tests, logging and laboratory petrophysics on named plugs and intervals, and the counts agree across text and figures (11 plugs at three stress stages, 7 + 4 intervals of 0.73 m, interval bars and sampling lines in Fig. 2 matching Fig. 6 to 8); this lets a reader trace each value to a depth, facies and sample (PP-13, PP-16, PP-27, PP-34).
2. For facies 1 to 3 the laboratory and field gap is large: the highest F3 plug lies 5.5 to 5.7 decades below the fault-core interval values, far more than the stress-stage effect of a factor of 5 to 7 within one plug; the direction of the scale effect for matrix-dominated plugs is therefore not an artefact of the stress stages, even though its attribution to large fractures still needs the separation asked for in GB-05 (GB-04, GB-05, PP-33).
3. Figures and text agree in most quantitative checks (tunnel metres, borehole azimuths, interval positions, Vp and porosity extremes, static pore pressures in BO5, crosshole values, flow-dimension range), so the deviations found are local and can be corrected point by point (PP-12, PP-17, PP-29, PP-32, PP-42, PP-43; deviations in GB-28 and GB-37).
4. The manuscript discloses its main limits (stress field assumed from mini-fracs at TM1750 to TM2250, data from another campaign, packer overlap, mixed facies in BO6 intervals, WIP underestimating porosity, the facies 4 outlier in Sect. 5.1), and all 69 reference entries are cited with matching years; a reader can locate the weak points, although several consequences are not carried forward (PP-15, PP-40, PP-46; GB-04).

## Weaknesses

1. Several conclusions on the fault architecture rest on evidence that does not carry them: the main flow path through the core is drawn from two single crosshole tests whose values differ by a factor of 2.4 (GB-49), the compartmentalization is not seen in BO6 (GB-53), the clustering argument contradicts the Results (GB-33), the intrinsic network control is adopted from literature (GB-55), and the independence of volumes across the tunnel has no test behind it (GB-56); the conclusions should be based on the single-hole profiles, reworded as "consistent with", and supported by an interference test design.
2. The scale-effect result is stated more generally than the data allow: "consistently" lower laboratory values ignore the facies 4 plug (GB-04), scale is confounded with measurement principle, stress state and sample selection (GB-05, GB-12), Fig. 9 counts repeated readings as data points (GB-45), and the decrease at the largest scale depends on where the crosshole values are placed (GB-47); a paired plug and interval comparison at in-situ stress, Fig. 9 with independent units and a numeric scale axis in Fig. 10 would let the claim be checked.
3. Key claims and one stated aim are not tested against the reported data: the control of permeability by open fractures (GB-03), the selection of injection sites (GB-02), the facies assignment of the BO6 intervals (GB-32) and the P10 comparison between boreholes (GB-34); a per-interval table linking fracture counts, facies shares and permeability with a rank correlation, and either selection criteria or a narrower aim, would close these gaps.
4. The data basis cannot be rebuilt from the manuscript: field and laboratory evaluation steps, statistics and porosity derivation are undocumented (GB-17, GB-21, GB-24, GB-29, GB-30), campaigns are undated and the crosshole interval length is inconsistent (GB-22, GB-35), the sample base is not reported (GB-15), several values differ between text and figures (GB-20, GB-37, GB-40, GB-41), code is available only on request and the repository content is unspecified (GB-58, GB-59), and four cited works are missing from the references (GB-44); per-test and per-sample tables, a deposited code archive and completed references would make the results reproducible.
