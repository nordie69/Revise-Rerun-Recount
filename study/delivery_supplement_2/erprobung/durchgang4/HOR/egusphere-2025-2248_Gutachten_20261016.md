## Summary

The manuscript reports a single-site survey study, described as mixed methods, on how students at the University Centre in Svalbard (UNIS) perceive Virtual Field Experiences (VFEs) delivered through the web-based VR Svalbard platform, which combines drone photogrammetry, photospheres, digital outcrop models and map layers into Virtual Field Guides (VFGs, n = 6) and Virtual Field Tours (VFTs, n = 175). Students of eight Bachelor, Master and PhD courses run in 2023 and 2024 were invited to one voluntary web survey after their exposure to the VFEs (Table 1: 131 enrolments, 116 distinct students); 69 consented and 66 completed it. The instruments are a 16-item Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) questionnaire on a 7-point scale (Appendix A; means of 10 items reported, 5.4 to 6.0, n = 54), a 28-item 5-point questionnaire on educational impact in a BEFORE, an AFTER and an untitled block (Appendix B; 41 pre-field and 52 post-field respondents), three usage questions and open-ended comments, analysed descriptively. Key reported results are 86 % platform use, 83 % agreement with better geographic orientation, 85 % with the value of the multi-scale map approach and 75 % with feeling better prepared. The Discussion answers two research questions: how students perceive the usefulness of digital field representations, and how VFEs contribute to managing the novelty space.

## Strengths

1. The questionnaires are reproduced item by item in Appendices A and B with the response options as administered. A reader can therefore check the quoted item wordings and the reported constructs against the instrument, and most quotations and data sources could be verified in this way (PP-14, PP-30); the same reproduction is what makes the inconsistencies in GB-30 and GB-34 traceable.
2. Fig. 7 gives the full response distribution of 16 items with n per row. Its row sums lie within rounding, six of the seven combined agreement shares quoted in Sections 4.2.4 to 4.2.6 match it, and means recomputed from it reproduce the Fig. 6 values to one decimal, so the post-field percentages can be checked by any reader (PP-25, PP-26, PP-27).
3. Each research question is stated in the Introduction and answered in its own Discussion subsection (5.1, 5.2), and the Conclusions' statements on preparedness, spatial awareness and post-field reflection can be traced to values in the Results (PP-34, PP-37).
4. The account of barriers to field-based learning in the Introduction rests on sources that, where they could be read, support the statements made (Cliffe 2017, Malm et al. 2020, Pugsley et al. 2024), which gives the motivation of the study a documented basis (PP-01, PP-02, PP-04); the exceptions are listed in GB-03 and GB-04.

## Weaknesses

1. The before and after comparison, announced as one of the four result themes, does not compare two time points: the survey was given once after exposure, the pre-field panel of Fig. 6 contains items of the untitled third block with n = 52, and the stated higher value and engagement after fieldwork are contradicted by Fig. 4 and Fig. 6 (GB-18, GB-32, GB-37). Assign every item to its block, time and n, describe the BEFORE block as retrospective, and report matched items as paired data or withdraw the comparative claim.
2. Several headline claims go beyond what was measured: research question 2 on the novelty space has no instrument, the views on non-replacement and inclusivity are attributed to students without supporting items, and self-reported perceptions are stated as demonstrated effects (GB-02, GB-10, GB-44, GB-45). Restrict Abstract, Discussion and Conclusions to the perceptions that were collected, or add measures that operationalise novelty together with a comparison condition.
3. The reporting of the instruments is incomplete and partly inconsistent: no Behavioral Intention value is shown although the TAM conclusion rests on it, nine Appendix B items are not reported, the scales are described with wrong ranges and anchors, quotations differ from the items, and three values in the text differ from the figures (GB-28, GB-29, GB-30, GB-33, GB-34, GB-38). Add a complete item table with block, n, mean and distribution, and quote every item verbatim.
4. Sample accounting, possible bias and documentation need to be completed: the total of 131 counts one cohort twice, section sizes and percentage bases are not reconciled, self-selection and the authors' role as developers of the platform are not discussed, parts of the instrument and the data are not available, and twelve cited works are missing from the reference list while several cited sources do not support the statements attributed to them (GB-06, GB-20, GB-21, GB-24, GB-25, GB-43, GB-47, GB-50). Add a respondent flow, a limitations paragraph, a complete data availability statement and a checked reference list.
